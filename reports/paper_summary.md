# Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability

Rabanser, Kapoor, Kirgis, Liu, Utpala, and Narayanan argue that agent evaluation needs to move beyond a single success-rate number. Their starting point is the gap between rising benchmark accuracy and failures seen in deployed agents: agents can be capable on average while still behaving unpredictably, failing under small changes, misjudging their own likelihood of success, or causing failures with very different levels of harm. The paper frames reliability as an empirical property of agent behavior rather than a vague safety slogan.

The authors borrow from safety-critical engineering and divide reliability into four dimensions. Consistency asks whether the same task produces stable outcomes, trajectories, and resource use across repeated runs. Robustness asks whether performance degrades gracefully under prompt changes, environment changes, or tool faults. Predictability asks whether an agent can meaningfully distinguish cases it is likely to solve from cases it is likely to fail, using calibration, discrimination, and Brier-style scoring. Safety asks whether failures obey constraints and whether harms are bounded when constraints are violated.

A central contribution is the metric suite. The paper proposes twelve concrete metrics grouped under those four dimensions and explains how they complement task accuracy. This is important because capability and reliability are intentionally separated: a high-accuracy agent can be unreliable if its successes vary from run to run or if its failures are rare but severe. Conversely, a lower-accuracy agent may still be useful within a narrower operating envelope if its behavior is predictable and failures are easy to route to humans.

The empirical study evaluates 14 agentic models on two benchmarks: GAIA, which stresses open-ended multi-step assistance, and tau-bench, which simulates customer-service workflows with tool use and constraints. The main finding is that reliability gains lag behind capability gains. Newer models improve on accuracy, but the paper reports only modest improvement in overall reliability, with especially persistent issues in consistency and predictability. The results also show that reliability can vary by benchmark: structured environments show clearer progress than open-ended tasks.

The dimension-level findings are the most useful part of the paper for this assignment. Outcome consistency remains weak, meaning an agent that solves a task once may fail on a rerun. Trajectory analysis shows a "what but not when" pattern: agents may use similar action types while ordering them differently. Prompt and environment perturbations reveal that small interface or wording changes can still damage performance. Predictability remains limited because agents are not reliably calibrated about when they will succeed.

The recommendations follow directly from those findings. Benchmarks should become multi-run, perturbation-aware, and periodically refreshed instead of fixed one-shot leaderboards. Agent architectures should be optimized for reliability properties, not only raw pass rate. Production evaluation should track resource variation, confidence quality, failure severity, and sensitivity to changing tools or data schemas. This repository's message-count analysis is a small example of that broader philosophy: a leaderboard rank is easier to interpret when paired with trajectory depth and cost signals.

My main takeaway is that the paper gives a vocabulary for judging agents as systems. For coding agents, a model that reaches a high resolved rate through very long, expensive, or unstable trajectories may be less attractive than the headline number suggests. Reliability work should therefore measure both the final answer and the path taken to reach it.
